# Built Environment as a Sustainability System

## Definition

The built environment comprises human-made settings that structure human activity, from individual shelters and buildings to neighborhoods and metropolitan areas. The guide presents it as connected to natural, economic, and cultural environments rather than as an isolated object. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 010|PDF page 10]]

## Why it matters

Buildings and communities require resources to create and operate, and their energy, water, and material demands affect the environment and human health. A useful sustainability boundary therefore extends beyond equipment inside a building. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 010|PDF page 10]]

## How it works

The guide moves between several nested scales: building, site, neighborhood, community, and metropolitan area. Decisions at one scale can shape opportunities and impacts at another. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 010|PDF page 10]]

## LEED relevance

The guide establishes built-environment scope before introducing later green-building strategies and the LEED certification system. This note is a framing concept, not a statement of a LEED prerequisite or credit. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 010|PDF page 10]]

## Application to MEP and piping engineering

> [!note] Practical engineering interpretation
> This section is an engineering interpretation, not a LEED requirement stated by the source.

- Define the system boundary before optimizing equipment: building loads, utility interfaces, water flows, site systems, and user behavior may interact.
- Record which effects are inside the design scope and which depend on site, infrastructure, operations, or community decisions.
