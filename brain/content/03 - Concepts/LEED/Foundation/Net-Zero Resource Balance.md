# Net-Zero Resource Balance

## Definition

The guide describes net-zero as using no more of a resource than a project can produce over the stated balance. Its examples address energy, carbon, water, and waste with different accounting boundaries. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 023|PDF page 23]]

## Why it matters

Net-zero turns a general reduction goal into a balance between resource use and production, sequestration, offsetting, precipitation, reuse, recycling, or composting. The source also states that not every project can reach these performance levels. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 023|PDF page 23]]

## How it works

Each resource requires its own boundary. The guide's energy example balances grid electricity drawn and on-site renewable electricity contributed; its water example relates use to on-site precipitation; its waste example depends on diversion through reuse, recycling, or composting. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 023|PDF page 23]]

## LEED relevance

The source presents net-zero examples within its discussion of moving beyond green. It does not provide current LEED definitions, time periods, calculation protocols, or thresholds for these balances. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 023|PDF page 23]]

## Application to MEP and piping engineering

> [!note] Practical engineering interpretation
> This section is an engineering interpretation, not a LEED requirement stated by the source.

- State the resource, boundary, time period, included flows, and treatment of imports and exports before using a net-zero label.
- Keep energy, carbon, water, and waste balances separate unless a documented method relates them.
