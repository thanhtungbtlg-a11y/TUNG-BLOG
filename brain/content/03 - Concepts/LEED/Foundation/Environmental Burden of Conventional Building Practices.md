# Environmental Burden of Conventional Building Practices

## Definition

The environmental burden of conventional building practices is the combined effect of land clearing, material extraction and transport, construction, building operations, waste generation, and travel to and from buildings. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 010|PDF page 10]] [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 011|PDF page 11]]

## Why it matters

The guide connects these practices to habitat loss, water and air pollution, toxic releases, greenhouse-gas emissions, energy and water demand, waste streams, and human-health effects. Treating only one impact can leave other substantial effects untouched. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 011|PDF page 11]]

## How it works

Impacts accumulate across a project rather than arising from one component alone. The guide groups them across resources, site change, materials, operations, and transportation. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 010|PDF page 10]] [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 011|PDF page 11]]

## LEED relevance

The source describes LEED achievement areas that include energy savings, water efficiency, carbon-dioxide emissions reduction, indoor environmental quality, and resource stewardship. It does not supply current thresholds in this introductory discussion. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 015|PDF page 15]]

## Application to MEP and piping engineering

> [!note] Practical engineering interpretation
> This section is an engineering interpretation, not a LEED requirement stated by the source.

- Review energy, water, refrigerant, material, discharge, construction, and operating effects as connected categories.
- Avoid declaring an option better from one metric until significant effects shifted to another category have been considered.
