# Triple Bottom Line for the Built Environment

## Definition

The triple bottom line evaluates long-term effects on people or social capital, the planet or natural capital, and profit or economic capital. The guide applies the lens to all stakeholders affected by a built-environment project, not only the owner. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 012|PDF page 12]]

## Why it matters

A project can perform well in one dimension and still be unsustainable in another. The source gives examples in which owner savings, low carbon impact, or economic activity do not compensate for harm to occupants, workers, species, or communities. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 012|PDF page 12]]

## How it works

The decision frame expands the list of costs and benefits, lengthens the time horizon, and asks who receives each benefit or burden. Research and metrics then support comparison across the three forms of capital. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 012|PDF page 12]] [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 013|PDF page 13]]

## LEED relevance

The guide presents the triple bottom line as a sustainability concept for the built environment. It does not present the three-part lens here as a LEED score or credit calculation. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 012|PDF page 12]]

## Application to MEP and piping engineering

> [!note] Practical engineering interpretation
> This section is an engineering interpretation, not a LEED requirement stated by the source.

- Compare system options across occupant outcomes, environmental effects, capital cost, operating cost, and effects on other stakeholders.
- Make trade-offs explicit when an option improves one dimension while burdening another.
