# Water Balance Approach for Buildings

## Definition

A water balance approach maps site supply, end uses, losses, wastewater, and rainwater so demand can be reduced first and remaining nonpotable demands can be matched to suitable alternative supplies.

## Sourced Facts

- The guide recommends assessing existing water resources, demand-reduction opportunities, and alternative supplies through an integrative process. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 068|PDF p. 68; guide p. 61]]
- It describes an efficiency-first sequence: reduce total and potable-water demand before considering nonpotable or alternative supplies. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 069|PDF p. 69; guide p. 62]]
- A water end-use profile helps identify major uses and evaluate strategies; submetering can track trends, fixture performance, and leaks. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 069|PDF p. 69; guide p. 62]] [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 070|PDF p. 70; guide p. 63]]
- Rainwater, graywater, or reclaimed water may serve suitable nonpotable uses where regulations permit. [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 069|PDF p. 69; guide p. 62]] [[02 - Sources/PDF/leed-coreconcepts-strategies-3rd-edition/pages/Page 070|PDF p. 70; guide p. 63]]

## Interpretation

> [!note] Interpretation
> Build the water model by end use, not by a single whole-building total. Sequence measures as avoid demand, improve efficiency, substitute fit-for-purpose water, meter, and correct. Verify local health and plumbing rules before assuming an alternative source is permissible.
